## DOC001 — Billing Statement vs. Negotiable Instrument

- **Script ID:** DOC001
- **Content Pillar:** Document literacy
- **Target Viewer:** Consumer who has heard that a billing statement is a negotiable instrument
- **Primary Question:** Is a billing statement the same as a negotiable instrument?
- **Hook (0–3s):** "A billing statement is not automatically a negotiable instrument."
- **Problem (3–15s):** Some theories claim you can treat a monthly statement like a check or bond. That is not generally true.
- **Rule or Principle (15–40s):** A negotiable instrument has specific legal requirements, such as being payable to order or bearer and containing an unconditional promise to pay a sum certain. A routine billing statement usually does not meet those requirements.
- **Example (40–60s):** A credit card statement says "payment due." It is asking for payment. It is not promising to pay you, and it is not written to be negotiable.
- **Limit or Exception (60–70s):** Some documents can be negotiable instruments, but that depends on their wording, not on the label "statement."
- **Action Step (70–80s):** If someone tells you a statement is a negotiable instrument, compare the document to the legal definition.

## MYTH001 — Why “Accepted for Value” Is Not Automatic Payment

- **Script ID:** MYTH001
- **Content Pillar:** Myth correction
- **Target Viewer:** Consumer exposed to "accepted for value" theories
- **Primary Question:** Can you discharge an account merely by endorsement?
- **Hook (0–3s):** "Writing 'accepted for value' on a bill does not pay it."
- **Problem (3–15s):** Some promoters claim an endorsement cancels a debt or creates a treasury-account credit. Those theories are not supported by mainstream law.
- **Rule or Principle (15–40s):** Payment generally requires actual value or an agreement to satisfy an obligation in a recognized way. A notation on a statement does not create payment or discharge a valid debt.
- **Example (40–60s):** You write "accepted for value" on a credit card statement and mail it back. The creditor is not paid. The balance remains.
- **Limit or Exception (60–70s):** A creditor and debtor can agree to alternative payment terms, but that takes mutual agreement, not a unilateral notation.
- **Action Step (70–80s):** If you are told a notation discharges debt, ask for the statute, regulation, or court decision that says so.

## COURT001 — Court Complaint vs. Judgment

- **Script ID:** COURT001
- **Content Pillar:** Consumer-law fundamentals
- **Target Viewer:** Consumer who has been sued or fears being sued
- **Primary Question:** Is a court complaint the same as a judgment?
- **Hook (0–3s):** "A complaint is a lawsuit. A judgment is a decision."
- **Problem (3–15s):** Some people freeze when they are served with a complaint, thinking they have already lost. They have not.
- **Rule or Principle (15–40s):** A complaint is the document that starts a lawsuit. A judgment is a court's formal decision after the process. They are different stages.
- **Example (40–60s):** You receive a summons and complaint. You still have time to respond, and the court has not yet ruled against you.
- **Limit or Exception (60–70s):** Deadlines and procedures vary by jurisdiction and court. Missing a deadline can have serious consequences.
- **Action Step (70–80s):** Read the summons carefully, note the response deadline, and consider speaking with a qualified professional promptly.

## EVID003 — What an Affidavit Can and Cannot Prove

- **Script ID:** EVID003
- **Content Pillar:** Evidence building
- **Target Viewer:** Consumer who has received an affidavit in a collection or court matter
- **Primary Question:** Does an affidavit become true just because it is unanswered?
- **Hook (0–3s):"** "An affidavit is someone's sworn statement. It is not automatic proof."
- **Problem (3–15s):** Some people believe that if they do not respond to an affidavit, everything in it becomes true. That is not how evidence works.
- **Rule or Principle (15–40s):** An affidavit is a sworn written statement. Its weight depends on who made it, what they know, and whether it is supported by other evidence. Silence alone does not make it true.
- **Example (40–60s):** A collector submits an affidavit saying you owe $5,000. The affiant may or may not have personal knowledge of your account. You can challenge it.
- **Limit or Exception (60–70s):** Court rules on affidavits vary. In some proceedings, an unchallenged affidavit may carry weight; in others, it does not.
- **Action Step (70–80s):** Ask who signed the affidavit, what they personally know, and what documents support it.

## CREDIT001 — Credit Bureau Dispute vs. Collector Dispute

- **Script ID:** CREDIT001
- **Content Pillar:** Consumer-law fundamentals
- **Target Viewer:** Consumer confused about whether to dispute with a credit bureau or a collector
- **Primary Question:** Is disputing with a credit bureau the same as disputing with a collector?
- **Hook (0–3s):"** "Credit bureau dispute and collector dispute are two different things."
- **Problem (3–15s):** Some people think a credit dispute letter to a bureau also disputes the underlying debt with the collector. It does not necessarily.
- **Rule or Principle (15–40s):** A credit bureau dispute asks the bureau to investigate what appears on your credit report. A collector dispute challenges the validity or amount of a debt directly with the collector. They have different rules and deadlines.
- **Example (40–60s):** You dispute an account with Experian. Experian contacts the furnisher. Meanwhile, the collector may still send you letters unless you separately dispute with the collector.
- **Limit or Exception (60–70s):** The Fair Credit Reporting Act and Fair Debt Collection Practices Act create different rights. State laws may add more.
- **Action Step (70–80s):** Decide what you want to fix: the report entry, the debt claim, or both. Then send the right dispute to the right party.

## CFPB001 — Three CFPB Complaint Mistakes

- **Script ID:** CFPB001
- **Content Pillar:** Enforcement pathways
- **Target Viewer:** Consumer considering a CFPB complaint
- **Primary Question:** What mistakes weaken a CFPB complaint?
- **Hook (0–3s):** "Three mistakes that weaken CFPB complaints."
- **Problem (3–15s):** Consumers file complaints but do not get the result they want. Often the complaint itself was unclear or missing proof.
- **Rule or Principle (15–40s):** Strong CFPB complaints usually: (1) name the exact issue, (2) include dates and documents, and (3) state the resolution you want.
- **Example (40–60s):** "Company X keeps calling me" is weak. "Company X called me on June 1, 3, and 5 after I sent a cease-communication letter on May 28. Here are the call logs" is stronger.
- **Limit or Exception (60–70s):** The CFPB forwards complaints to companies and can take supervisory or enforcement action, but it does not guarantee a specific outcome for every individual complaint.
- **Action Step (70–80s):** Before filing, gather your records, write a concise timeline, and clearly state what you want the company to do.

## PRIV001 — How to Redact Consumer Records Safely

- **Script ID:** PRIV001
- **Content Pillar:** Evidence building
- **Target Viewer:** Consumer preparing to share records with an advocate or professional
- **Primary Question:** How do you redact sensitive information before sharing documents?
- **Hook (0–3s):** "Before you share any document, redact first."
- **Problem (3–15s):** People email screenshots or documents with full account numbers, SSNs, and addresses visible.
- **Rule or Principle (15–40s):** Redaction means permanently covering or removing sensitive data before sharing. Black boxes, blurring, or opaque markers are common methods. Do not rely on translucent highlights.
- **Example (40–55s):** You have a collection letter. Before posting or sending it, cover your full account number, the collector's internal codes if they identify you, your address, and any signature.
- **Limit or Exception (55–60s):** Keep the date, company name, and the part of the letter that shows the claim if you need it for the dispute.
- **Action Step (60–70s):** Use the redaction checklist in the free Starter Sheet before sharing any record.

## EVID004 — How to Assemble an Exhibit Index

- **Script ID:** EVID004
- **Content Pillar:** Evidence building
- **Target Viewer:** Consumer preparing materials for a professional or agency review
